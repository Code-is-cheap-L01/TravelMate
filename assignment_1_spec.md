# Assignment 1 - Product Discovery & Foundation

## 1. Objective

In this assignment, your team will establish the product foundation for the mobile application that will be developed throughout the course.

Before designing the complete interface or starting implementation, your team must first demonstrate that:

- the problem is specific and worth investigating;
- the target users and usage context are identifiable;
- the project direction is supported by initial evidence rather than team opinion alone;
- mobile is an appropriate platform for the proposed product;
- the important assumptions behind the idea have been identified and investigated;
- the MVP is derived from evidence and is feasible within the course;
- the team has a clear project structure for continuing to the next development stages.

The purpose of this assignment is **not to prove that your original idea is correct**.

The purpose is to learn enough about the problem, users, and existing alternatives so that your team can make a reasonable decision about **what should actually be built next**.

---

## 2. Assignment Requirements

### 2.1. Project Direction

Describe the current project direction clearly.

Your documentation should identify:

- the problem your team intends to address;
- the target users;
- the context in which the problem occurs;
- how users currently deal with the problem;
- the proposed direction of the solution.

The problem should be specific enough that your team can identify realistic users and collect meaningful evidence.

Avoid broad descriptions such as:

> Students have difficulty studying.

or:

> People need a better shopping application.

A better project direction identifies a specific user, context, and problem behavior.

If your team changed the original idea after class discussion or initial research, briefly explain what changed and why.

Changing direction based on better evidence is acceptable.

---

### 2.2. Mobile Product Rationale

Explain why the proposed product should be a **mobile application**.

Do not assume that every software solution needs a mobile app.

Your explanation should consider:

- the task users need to perform;
- the context in which they perform it;
- relevant mobile capabilities;
- relevant mobile constraints;
- and whether another platform could solve the same problem more appropriately.

Relevant mobile characteristics may include:

- mobility;
- short and frequent interactions;
- location and physical context;
- camera or microphone;
- notifications;
- sensors;
- personal-device usage;
- intermittent connectivity;
- offline or local behavior;
- other device-specific capabilities.

Do not add a mobile-only feature simply to justify the platform.

The question is:

> **Does mobile meaningfully improve the actual task or user context?**

The statement:

> “*We use mobile because this is a Mobile Application Development course.*”

is not a valid rationale.

---

### 2.3. Critical Assumptions

Every product idea contains assumptions.

Identify the assumptions that are most important to your project.

Examples may include assumptions about:

- whether the problem occurs frequently enough;
- whether the selected users actually experience the problem;
- how users currently behave;
- whether existing alternatives are insufficient;
- whether users would adopt the proposed workflow;
- whether required data or resources are accessible;
- whether a particular feature creates meaningful value.

Your team should then select the **most important uncertain assumption** to investigate first.

The selected assumption should:

- be specific;
- be testable;
- matter to the project;
- and be capable of changing a product decision if it turns out to be wrong.

Do not select an assumption only because it is easy to confirm.

---

### 2.4. Evidence Plan

For the selected critical assumption, define what your team needs to learn before collecting evidence.

Your evidence plan should include:

- the exact assumption or claim being investigated;
- why the assumption matters;
- what evidence already exists;
- what remains uncertain;
- the research question;
- the selected research method;
- who should participate or be observed;
- what evidence would support the assumption;
- what evidence would challenge the assumption;
- what result would leave the question unresolved.

Possible methods include:

- interviews;
- surveys;
- contextual observation;
- analysis of existing data;
- community or market research;
- simple experiments;
- task observation;
- or a combination of methods.

Choose the method based on **what you need to learn**.

Do not automatically create a Google Form for every question.

---

### 2.5. User and Market Research

Conduct appropriate research to investigate the problem and selected assumptions.

Your documentation should report:

- who participated or what sources were studied;
- how participants or sources were selected;
- the research method;
- when and how the research was conducted;
- important findings;
- contradictory findings where relevant;
- limitations of the collected evidence;
- and the implications for the project.

### About surveys

A larger number of responses does not automatically mean better evidence.

Survey quality depends on factors such as:

- whether respondents are relevant to the target users;
- how participants were recruited;
- what questions were asked;
- whether the questions are leading or biased;
- what the collected responses can reasonably support.

Do not treat a response-count target as a universal validation threshold.

### About user statements

Be careful about the difference between:

- what users say they did in the past;
- what users say they would do in the future;
- what your team directly observes;
- and what users actually do when interacting with a product.

These are different forms of evidence.

---

### 2.6. Existing Solutions and Alternatives

Investigate the current solution landscape.

Analyze at least **five relevant competing or related solutions**.

These may include:

- direct competitors;
- similar applications;
- indirect competitors;
- websites;
- existing services;
- manual workflows;
- spreadsheets;
- social media groups;
- messaging applications;
- or other ways users currently solve the problem.

For each relevant alternative, investigate aspects that help your team understand the problem and product opportunity.

Your analysis should lead to answers such as:

- What are users already using?
- What works well?
- What does not work well?
- What needs are already adequately addressed?
- What gaps remain?
- What could realistically differentiate your project?

Do not submit only screenshots or a feature comparison table.

The important part is **what your team learns from the comparison**.

---

### 2.7. Evidence Evaluation and Product Decision

After collecting evidence, decide what it means for the project.

For each important assumption investigated, state the current decision:

- **Keep** - current direction is sufficiently supported for the next stage;
- **Revise** - retain the general direction but change part of it;
- **Reject** - evidence suggests that the assumption or direction should not be retained;
- **Investigate further** - available evidence is not sufficient for the decision.

An **inconclusive result is acceptable**.

Do not reinterpret weak evidence simply to make the original idea appear correct.

Your documentation should explain:

- what you found;
- what limitations remain;
- what decision you made;
- and what changed in the project because of that evidence.

---

### 2.8. Business Canvas

Complete the **course-specific 3×3 Business Canvas**.

Use the following structure:

| Problem         | Solution  | Value   |
| --------------- | --------- | ------- |
| Customer / User | Advantage | Metrics |
| Channels        | Costs     | Revenue |

The canvas should not be treated as nine unrelated boxes.

Consider the relationships among the elements, especially:

> **Problem → Customer/User → Channels**

> **Solution → Advantage → Costs**

> **Value → Metrics → Revenue**

Your canvas should reflect the product direction **after considering the evidence collected by the team**.

Important claims should be supported by evidence where possible.

If an item is still uncertain, identify it as an assumption instead of presenting it as a confirmed fact.

---

### 2.9. MVP Scope and Prioritization

Define the Minimum Viable Product that your team intends to continue into the design stage.

The MVP should represent the smallest coherent product that can deliver and test the core proposed value.

Identify:

- the primary user goal;
- the core value path;
- the minimum set of required features;
- features intentionally excluded from the MVP;
- and major risks or unresolved assumptions.

Use an **Impact–Effort Matrix** to support feature prioritization.

Do not use the matrix mechanically.

Explain why important features belong in the MVP.

### Important

More features do not mean a better project.

The MVP should be small enough that the team can later:

- design it properly;
- implement it;
- test it;
- release it;
- and obtain meaningful feedback.

If your MVP requires too many independent features before users receive any value, reduce the scope.

---

### 2.10. User Stories

Create user stories for the significant MVP features.

Use the following format:

> **As a [user], I want to [goal], so that [value].**

The story should describe user value, not a programming task.

Example:

**Poor**

> As a developer, I want to implement an authentication API.

**Better**

> As a returning user, I want to access my account quickly so that I can continue my previous activity.

User stories should be traceable to the product evidence:

> **Evidence → User Need → Requirement → User Story → MVP Feature**

A feature should exist because it supports a meaningful user need or an explicit hypothesis.

---

### 2.11. Critical User Flows

Create user flow diagrams for at least **two important MVP flows**.

Select flows that represent the core value of the proposed product.

Each flow should show:

- starting point;
- major user actions;
- important decisions;
- expected outcome;
- relevant alternative or failure paths when appropriate.

The flows should describe **product and interaction logic**.

Detailed UI design is not required yet.

Do not spend Assignment 1 creating polished application screens.

---

### 2.12. Project Foundation

Create a GitHub Organization for your team.

All members must join the organization.

The organization will be used throughout the course for:

- source code;
- project documentation;
- project planning;
- and collaboration.

Create a GitHub Project that represents the team's real development work.

The project should make the following information visible:

- backlog;
- planned tasks;
- task ownership;
- current task status;
- milestones;
- project progress.

A simple workflow such as:

> **Backlog → To Do → In Progress → Review → Done**

is acceptable.

Your team may adjust the workflow if another structure better represents your development process.

Tasks should be derived from the actual MVP and project plan.

Do not create fake tasks only to make the board appear complete.

## 4. Traceability

The major product decisions should connect to each other.

A good project should be able to demonstrate a chain such as:

> **Evidence**
> **→ User Need**
> **→ Requirement**
> **→ User Story**
> **→ MVP Feature**
> **→ User Flow**
> **→ Development Task**

Do not treat each assignment artifact as an independent checklist item.

For example:

- a user story should correspond to a real user need;
- an MVP feature should correspond to a user story or explicit hypothesis;
- a GitHub task should correspond to work actually required by the MVP.

Your project documentation should allow the lecturer to follow this reasoning.

---

## 5. What Is Not Required Yet

Assignment 1 focuses on **discovery, evidence, product decisions, and project foundation**.

The following are not required yet:

- complete UI design;
- polished wireframes;
- complete Figma screens;
- design system;
- high-fidelity prototype;
- complete mobile implementation;
- backend implementation;
- production deployment.

Do not spend most of your time making the project look finished before determining whether the team is building the right product.

The next assignments will continue from the decisions and evidence produced here.

| Existing Solutions & Alternatives         | 10%      |

###  

### Problem, Target Users & Project Direction - 10%

Evaluation considers:

- problem specificity;
- clarity of the target users;
- understanding of the usage context;
- understanding of current user behavior;
- and overall coherence of the project direction.

---

### Mobile Product Rationale - 10%

Evaluation considers:

- whether mobile is meaningfully justified;
- connection between user context and mobile capabilities;
- awareness of mobile constraints;
- and whether the team considered reasonable alternatives.

A generic justification receives limited credit.

---

### Critical Assumptions & Evidence Plan - 15%

Evaluation considers:

- quality of identified assumptions;
- importance of the selected critical assumption;
- clarity of the research question;
- suitability of the selected method;
- participant/source selection;
- and whether the evidence plan could realistically change a project decision.

---

### User / Market Research & Evidence Quality - 20%

Evaluation considers:

- relevance of evidence;
- appropriateness of the research method;
- target-user fit;
- research-question quality;
- transparency of data collection;
- awareness of limitations and bias;
- and quality of interpretation.

The number of survey responses alone is not a grading criterion.

---

### Existing Solutions & Alternatives - 10%

Evaluation considers:

- quality and relevance of the selected alternatives;
- understanding of the current solution landscape;
- depth of analysis;
- and useful insights derived from the research.

---

### Evidence Evaluation & Product Decisions - 10%

Evaluation considers whether the team:

- distinguishes evidence from assumptions;
- considers contradictory or incomplete evidence;
- reaches reasonable conclusions;
- documents limitations;
- and changes product decisions when appropriate.

The assignment does not reward teams for proving that every original assumption was correct.

---

### Business Canvas - 5%

Evaluation considers:

- consistency of the 3×3 Business Canvas;
- relationships among its elements;
- connection to evidence;
- and clarity of the proposed value.

---

### MVP Scope, User Stories & User Flows - 15%

Evaluation considers:

- feasibility of the MVP;
- quality of prioritization;
- completeness of the core value path;
- connection between evidence, needs, stories, and features;
- and clarity of at least two critical user flows.

Visual polish is not evaluated here.

---

### Project Foundation & Traceability - 5%

Evaluation considers:

- GitHub Organization setup;
- team participation;
- useful documentation;
- meaningful project tasks;
- task ownership;
- milestones;
- and traceability between product decisions and planned work.

---

## 8. Important Notes

Your project direction is **not frozen** after Assignment 1.

You may revise:

- the problem;
- target users;
- assumptions;
- value proposition;
- MVP;
- user stories;
- or other product decisions

when new evidence justifies the change.

Document significant changes and explain why they were made.

Changing an idea after learning something is part of the process.

Do not hide contradictory evidence simply because it makes the project more difficult.

The important question is not:

> **Can we prove that our idea is good?**

The important questions are:

> - **What do we currently believe?**
> - **What evidence do we need?**
> - **What did we actually learn?**
> - **What should we build because of what we learned?**
